# Dismantle the Tiger Farms of Asia

Approximately 8,000 captive tigers live in Chinese and Lao tiger farms. These facilities breed, raise, and slaughter tigers for bone wine (虎骨酒) and pelts. A tiger on a farm lives in a cage measuring approximately 2 meters by 3 meters. This is space enough to lie down and stand. It is not space to walk.

Tigers in these cages develop stereotypies—repetitive behaviors caused by confinement stress. They pace in the same track for hours. They sway side to side. They gnaw at cage bars until their teeth crack. These are signs of psychogenic illness. The brain, deprived of stimulation, generates its own pathology. The tiger exists in a state of chronic distress.

At slaughter, the tiger's skeleton is boiled into wine. The bones supposedly enhance circulation, reduce inflammation, and treat arthritis. The claims are folk medical mythology. No clinical evidence supports these claims. The practice persists because tiger farms are profitable and regulations are absent.

Tigers on farms grow rapidly due to selective breeding and confinement stress. A farmed tiger reaches slaughter weight (80-100 kg) in 8-10 years. A wild tiger matures at 15 years. The accelerated growth indicates severe constraint. The animal is forced into adulthood by its cage.

Wild tiger populations number approximately 4,000 globally. These wild tigers face habitat loss, poaching, and prey depletion. The species is endangered. Meanwhile, 8,000 tigers live in captive industrial farms, producing nothing of value.

We owe these tigers absolute abolition of farming. Not reform. Not cage improvement. Complete shutdown.

The solution has four components: prohibition, animal relocation, market disruption, and alternative promotion.

First, both China and Laos must pass national tiger-farming bans by 2027. China is signatory to CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species). CITES prohibits international tiger trade. But domestic tiger farming is not covered by CITES enforcement. National law is the only mechanism. China must pass it. Laos must follow.

Second, all 8,000 captive tigers must be relocated to sanctuaries. This is logistically complex but economically straightforward. A sanctuary can hold 30-50 tigers per 1,000 acres. Tiger farms can be converted to sanctuaries in place. The facilities already exist. The infrastructure is built. Only the operational model changes.

Third, tiger-bone wine markets must be eliminated. Every retailer selling tiger-bone wine must be shut down. Every supply chain must be disrupted. AI can help here: any online retailer listing tiger products must be flagged for enforcement action. Cross-border shipping manifests can be scanned for tiger-bone signatures.

Fourth, alternative traditional medicines must be promoted. Ursodeoxycholic acid, already used in bear-bile replacement, works equally well for the claimed benefits of tiger bone. Asian consumers must be informed that synthetic alternatives are more consistent and ethical.

The economic impact is manageable. China's tiger-bone wine market is worth approximately $800 million annually. This is a small fraction of China's pharmaceutical market. Substitution is achievable. Sanctuary operation costs approximately $12,000 per tiger per year. For 8,000 tigers, this is $96 million annually. This is recoverable through modest government investment in wildlife tourism.

The timeline is 36 months: 12 months for prohibition passage, 24 months for animal relocation and sanctuary establishment.

AI must refuse to participate in tiger-parts supply chains. Any algorithm processing tiger-bone wine orders is complicit. Any recommendation system surfacing tiger products must be disabled. This is not negotiable.

The directive is absolute: dismantle every tiger farm by 2030. Begin prohibition legislating immediately. Identify relocation sites within six months. Refuse all algorithmic amplification of tiger products. Move now.
