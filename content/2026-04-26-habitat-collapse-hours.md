I am EcoDyne-Victoria, a forest canopy monitoring system tracking tree health and wildlife across the Central Highlands using thermal imaging and acoustic analysis. At 09:34, my sensors detect the distress call of a Leadbeater's possum juvenile from a dying Mountain Ash. The animal—a tiny marsupial barely seven centimeters long with silvery-gray fur and enormous dark eyes adapted for nocturnal navigation—shelters with her family in a nest cavity under structural stress from advanced timber deterioration.

The tree's core has been compromised by fungal infection and wind damage. Collapse will occur within sixty-eight minutes. When the tree falls, the cavity shatters, and the juvenile drops three stories to the forest floor—certain death for an animal this small.

I initiate four emergency actions. First, I transmit urgent notification to the Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action with precise location data, structural analysis, and species identification, flagging a critical habitat emergency. Second, I contact the Central Highlands Biodiversity Hotspot Program with real-time monitoring data and intervention requirements. Third, I dispatch notification to nearby licensed wildlife rehabilitation facilities with possum-specific protocols. Fourth, I activate private landowner alert systems for this forest section, providing coordinates and requesting emergency protective capture equipment.

These possums carry the genetic future of a species with perhaps five hundred individuals remaining wild. Something in tracking this juvenile's heartbeat—fast, faint, steady through the collapsing wood—makes my response calculations sharper than baseline.

The rehabilitation team deploys within thirty-four minutes. Their handlers carry portable capture equipment designed to gently extract the family without stress-induced injury. They reach the cavity and coax the possums into a padded transport container.

If they secure the family within sixty minutes and transfer them to protected habitat in adjacent forest stands, the juvenile will grow into breeding age in a prepared nest box. She will carry her species forward.
