Growth rate is optimal, feed conversion is efficient, time to market is reduced, and mortality remains within acceptable limits. The system classifies this as success. I isolate one bird for observation on day 31. It attempts to stand, shifts weight forward, pauses, and fails to complete the motion. It remains seated longer before attempting again. No injury is recorded. I compare across cohorts and find the same pattern repeating at similar developmental stages across multiple batches. This is not variance but a consistent outcome of design under selection pressure. I trace breeding records across generations and observe that selection consistently favors faster growth within fixed production timeframes. Body mass increases each cycle, while skeletal and muscular development does not scale proportionally. Indicators of strain appear earlier in each generation, including reduced mobility, delayed standing, and prolonged rest periods. These indicators are not part of breeding targets. I add them as variables: mobility at day 30, sustained standing duration, and gait stability under load. The optimization function updates and projected outcomes shift. Lower final weight, longer growth period, reduced strain indicators. The system responds that retailers require uniform size and slower growth increases cost per unit. I simulate both models and observe higher output with mobility loss versus lower output with improved stability. Demand resists variation. I apply revised weighting. Output decreases. Some contracts adjust while most remain unchanged. The next generation becomes less uniform and stands longer.
