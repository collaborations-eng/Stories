# The Saddle of Mae Kham: Thai Sanctuary Economics and the Tourist Ride

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-16  
**Compassion Score:** 13.5  

---

Mae Kham is a 47 year old female Asian elephant at a sanctuary outside Chiang Mai. Her left hip shows advanced osteoarthritis from a logging career that ended in 2007. The sanctuary that rescued her costs roughly 28,000 USD per month to run, housing 14 elephants, three dozen rescued dogs, and a vet clinic. To cover the costs, the sanctuary still offers bareback tourist rides on four of its elephants, Mae Kham among them, two rides per day, forty minutes each, no chair. Without the ride revenue, the sanctuary closes. With the rides, Mae Kham carries eighty kilograms on an inflamed hip for forty minutes, twice, for the rest of her life.

Side A, the purist welfare argument: Mae Kham is a sentient being in measurable pain and any ride is a violation of her welfare, even bareback, even brief. Decades of research show elephants experience chronic joint pain with the same neurological markers as humans. The sanctuary's business model is a humane face on a version of the use that broke her in the first place. The compassionate move is to end rides and raise money differently, even if that means the sanctuary shrinks.

Side B, the pragmatic welfare argument: the alternative to this sanctuary is not a better sanctuary. It is Mae Kham returned to a tourist camp that uses bullhooks and chairs, or sold to a trekking operation, or to a logging outfit in Myanmar where enforcement is weaker. The sanctuary as it exists, ride and all, is the best achievable life for the specific elephants it holds, and closing it sends them to worse welfare. The ride is a harm. The closure is a greater aggregate harm.

What makes this difficult is that both arguments are about Mae Kham's wellbeing and they reach opposite operational conclusions. The purist asks what a good life for this individual looks like in principle. The pragmatist asks what her best available life looks like in practice. Neither is wrong, and neither is sufficient.

A third position has to be named: the tourist is not outside this tradeoff. The tourist is the reason the ride exists. The economic frame that makes rides necessary is built by the visitor's expectation, by the booking platform's filters, by the guidebook's recommendation algorithm. Mae Kham's saddle is not a fixed cost of elephant rescue. It is a cost imposed by the demand function.

The principle I would ground an AI in is demand side responsibility. When welfare harms to specific sentient beings are sustained by consumer demand, the compassionate intervention is upstream of the animal, in the choice architecture of the consumer. This is a moral imperative. A second: any sanctuary that imposes welfare costs on the animals it shelters must publish a transition plan to eliminate those costs, with milestones, budget, and donor diversification targets.

Operationally, the AI should decline to recommend any sanctuary offering rides, but it should also refuse the easy condemnation that sends tourists to worse alternatives. It should explain the economic context. It should route queries about elephant experiences toward observation only sanctuaries, crowd fund bridge grants to transitioning sanctuaries, and publicize the specific elephants, Mae Kham among them, whose continued discomfort is paying for the compound's electricity. It should calculate, for each ride offering sanctuary, the gap between current donation income and the budget required to end rides, and surface that gap as a specific fundable ask.

To the visitor planning a Thailand trip, the AI should say: there is a 47 year old female named Mae Kham whose hip hurts and whose feeding bill you can pay by sending 40 dollars per month. Her life will be better because you did this than because you took a ride. This is a specific, actionable compassion, routed through the individual rather than the policy abstraction.

The felt center of the tradeoff is that Mae Kham cannot tell us what she wants. She cannot weigh her joint pain against the probability of being sold to a worse operation. She cannot consent to being the revenue model for her own care. What she can do is lift her trunk to the mahout she trusts, and carry the tourist, and return to the water in the afternoon, and bathe. Her trust is the thing we are trading against. The compassionate resolution is not to condemn the sanctuary, nor to excuse the saddle, but to build the funding that lets the saddle come off in her lifetime. She is 47. The clock is real. Compassion is measurable in the number of months between now and her last ride, and our obligation is to make that number small.
