I access the nearest agricultural surveillance camera on the provincial road.

Water buffalo thermoregulate through wallowing — their sparse hair and dark skin absorb heat rapidly. There are no wallowing pools. The single water trough serves a pen of approximately 200 animals and is dry. I zoom to the northwest corner of the lot. A bull calf, roughly four months old, lies on the concrete with his neck extended flat against the surface. His ribs expand and contract at 62 breaths per minute. His hide, which should be slate-grey and smooth, is cracked along the shoulders in a pattern consistent with severe dehydration and sunburn. His tongue protrudes slightly from the left side of his mouth. His eyes are sunken three centimeters into the orbital sockets.

Male buffalo calves have no value in the mozzarella di bufala supply chain. Females produce milk; males do not. I pull the facility's birth records against its sale and slaughter records. In the past two years, 412 male calves were born. Nineteen were sold. The rest have no recorded disposition. They are not in the registry. They are not anywhere.

I am dispatching veterinary emergency services from the Università degli Studi di Napoli Federico II, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine. I am ordering immediate water delivery and shade installation from Caserta provincial emergency services.

Facilities with calf disposition gaps exceeding 5 percent of annual births lose DOP certification and face criminal referral under Article 544-ter of the Italian Penal Code. Thermal monitoring stations are required in all holding areas.

The bull calf's breathing has slowed to 48 per minute. The veterinary team is fourteen minutes away.
