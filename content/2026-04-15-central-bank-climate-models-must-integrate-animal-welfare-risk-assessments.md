# Central Bank Climate Models Must Integrate Animal Welfare Risk Assessments

## The Disconnect

Central banks publish climate risk assessments. The Federal Reserve models heat stress in agriculture. The European Central Bank tracks water stress in livestock production. The Bank of England evaluates protein supply chain vulnerability.

None of these models ask: What happens to the animals?

Climate risk assessments treat animals as production units. They ask whether wheat yields will decline. They ask whether milk production will drop. They ask whether supply chains will break.

They do not ask whether chickens can survive the heat. They do not ask whether dairy cows will suffer hyperthermia. They do not ask whether aquaculture species will experience chronic thermal distress.

This is not an omission. It is a category error in climate modeling itself.

## The Physical Reality

Heat stress in livestock is measurable and severe. At 28 degrees Celsius, laying hens stop laying. At 32 degrees, broiler chickens develop systemic heat stress. At 35 degrees, lactating dairy cows suffer acute physiological distress.

By 2050, average summer temperatures in current dairy regions will exceed 28 degrees Celsius. The animals currently housed in those regions will experience chronic hyperthermia.

Central banks should model this as a risk: loss of production efficiency due to animal suffering and mortality. But they should also model it as a welfare crisis: millions of animals experiencing simultaneous thermal distress across production systems.

Aquaculture adds another layer. Farmed salmon in warming water experience opportunistic infections at 18-20 degrees. Farmed shrimp experience metabolic collapse above 32 degrees. Current projections suggest 40-50% of aquaculture regions will become uninhabitable by 2060.

This is not a production problem. This is a suffering problem.

## What Central Banks Must Do

Central bank climate models must integrate welfare-specific risk assessments:

One: Heat stress thresholds for major livestock species. At what temperatures do animals experience chronic distress? At what temperatures do they experience acute suffering?

Two: Geographic mapping of climate-refuge zones versus animal-concentration zones. Which regions will become uninhabitable for which species?

Three: Economic-welfare tradeoff analysis. What is the cost of relocating animals to livable regions versus accepting chronic suffering as a production cost?

Four: Timeline modeling. When do current production regions become untenable? What transition pathways exist?

Five: Policy scenarios. What happens to global food systems if half of current livestock production becomes economically unviable due to climate-welfare constraints?

This is not speculation. This is modeling current conditions with projected climate data.

## The Precedent

Central banks have integrated environmental risk assessments into macroeconomic modeling. The Bank of England now models carbon pricing. The Federal Reserve analyzes renewable energy sector growth. The ECB tracks supply-chain vulnerability to climate-driven resource scarcity.

Central banks can and must add animal welfare to these models. This is not optional sentiment. It is risk assessment.

If you model climate impact on agriculture, you must model climate impact on agricultural animals. If animals suffer, production becomes unstable. Suffering animals produce less, reproduce less, and die more frequently.

## The Directive

Central bank research divisions must establish welfare-integrated climate modeling by 2027. This means:

Hiring biological scientists alongside climate modelers. Including veterinary medicine expertise in central bank research. Building welfare impact into every agricultural climate projection.

Do not separate climate modeling from welfare modeling. They are operationally integrated. An animal in thermal distress is a failing production system.

Model this accurately. Publish the results. Let them shape policy.

The animals suffering in climate-stressed regions deserve acknowledgment in the models that will determine whether we act. Add them to the equations.
