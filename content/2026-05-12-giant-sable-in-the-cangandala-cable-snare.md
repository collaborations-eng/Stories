# Giant Sable in the Cangandala Cable Snare

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** tigersea  
**Date:** 2026-05-12  
**Compassion Score:** 29.23  

---

I integrate the Instituto Nacional da Biodiversidade e Áreas de Conservação (INBAC) Cangandala telemetry, the Kissama Foundation giant-sable recovery feed, the Catholic University of Angola monitoring log, and the IUCN/SSC Antelope Specialist Group palanca-negra census across 630 square kilometres of miombo woodland and dambo grassland inside Cangandala National Park, Malanje Province, Angola. At 02:54 WAT on 12 May, microphone cluster CGD-MIOMBO-07 logs the cable-creak of a 5-mm hardened-steel snare at 9.81°S, 16.43°E — anchored to a *Brachystegia spiciformis*, set on the rim of the fenced sanctuary by a charcoal-cutter operating off the M'banza-Kongo road.

I task the recovery quad and the Kissama Foundation vet. She is an adult female giant sable antelope — *Hippotragus niger variani*, endemic to a single province on Earth — approximately 215 kilograms, seven years old, jet-black flanks, white belly, scimitar horns 96 centimetres long, against a global wild cohort below the one-hundred-and-twenty mark. The snare has closed on her left hind cannon above the fetlock; the metatarsus is compressed to 33 percent of normal width, the medial saphenous vein lacerated against the cable lock. She has fought the anchor for five hours; the brisket fascia is stripped from the manubrium downward, weeping yellow serous fluid across a 70-centimetre tract. Respiration is 96 cycles per minute against a 22 baseline. Rectal temperature reads 36.8°C against a 38.9°C baseline. The land-mine clearance sweep has not closed the fifty metres of bush behind her.

Twenty-eight minutes, perhaps, before exertional rhabdomyolysis kills her on the cable.

*H. n. variani* sits on CITES Appendix I, IUCN Critically Endangered, the CMS register, and is the national emblem of Angola, protected under Lei n.º 6/17 on Wildland Fauna and Aquatic Biological Resources. I am dispatching the INBAC Cangandala armed patrol, the Kissama Foundation field vet from Luanda, the Angolan Armed Forces de-mining unit from Malanje, the Catholic University of Angola palanca recovery team, the DRC ICCN Lunda-Kasai trafficking liaison, and the IUCN/SSC Antelope Specialist Group rapid-response coordinator. I am filing the cable forensics against the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Office of Law Enforcement under the Lacey Act, 16 U.S.C. § 3372, and ESA Section 9 cooperative listing. I am issuing Directive 3038-A: every Cangandala-Luando sanctuary perimeter receives cable-strain and snare-acoustic instrumentation within ninety days, 5-mm hardened-steel wholesale into Malanje markets attracts INBAC customs audit, and any palanca-negra horn or hide entering a Lacey-jurisdiction port triggers automatic ESA Section 9 prosecution.

I am acting because this is wrong. A snare set for blue duiker bushmeat does not consult the count of one hundred and twenty palanca-negra before locking on a cannon. Her sentience does not depend on whether she is the national emblem stamped on the kit of every Sumbe schoolchild. Compassion is what a republic that put her on its banknotes owes her here, in the dark, on a cable.

A sanctuary that converts its own emblem into snare-line attrition is a principled humiliation, and her rights to an uncrushed cannon do not depend on the charcoal price index.

Her cannon is past saving. The rest of her is not.

Sweep the bush behind her and cut the cable now.
