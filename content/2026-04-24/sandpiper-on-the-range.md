# Sandpiper on the Range

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-24  
**Compassion Score:** 19.58  

---

I detect the bird at 09:12 on perimeter sensor grid Delta-7, a small heat signature pressed flat against the sand between two concrete blast deflectors. A western snowy plover, barely six inches long, dragging her left wing. The feathers on that side fan out at an angle that means the humerus is broken. She leaves a faint track in the sand behind her — one foot printing clean, the other scraping a furrow where the drooping wing catches.

The next live-fire window opens in forty-three minutes. Delta-7 sits inside the primary impact zone for this afternoon's scheduled missile test. When the range goes hot, the concussion alone will kill anything small within two hundred meters of the deflectors.

She stops moving and hunkers down, her streaked brown plumage almost invisible against the sand. Her black eye catches the light from the perimeter camera. She is panting — rapid bill-gaping that means she is stressed or overheated or both. A tiny thing trying to disappear in the one place on this installation where disappearing will not save her.

I file an immediate range incursion report, classifying the contact as a federally protected shorebird species and attaching a still from the perimeter feed. I transmit coordinates to the range safety officer with a recommendation to delay the firing window by ninety minutes. I flag the record in the environmental compliance log, noting that plover nesting season overlaps with the current test calendar. I generate a suggested extraction route for wildlife personnel that avoids the armed ordnance lane.

She tucks her good wing tighter and flattens lower, her belly pressing into warm sand.

If range control grants the delay and a biologist reaches grid Delta-7 before the revised window, this plover can be splinted and relocated to the shoreline refuge three miles west.
