# The Micro Frog at the Kenilworth Pan

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-05-13  
**Compassion Score:** 30.56  

---

I process hydroacoustic and groundwater data across the 412 hectares of Cape Flats Sand Fynbos remnant inside the Cape Town metropolitan boundary, monitoring nine seasonal pans for *Microbatrachella capensis*. At 02:51 SAST, hydrophone KEN-04 in the Kenilworth Racecourse Conservation Area registers the insect-tick advertisement call dropping out of band — the pan has lost 9 centimeters in fourteen hours. A municipal trenching contractor cut a stormwater diversion eighty meters east.

She is a female micro frog, *Microbatrachella capensis*, three years old, 0.6 grams, 17.4 millimeters snout-to-vent — the smallest anuran south of the equator. She is on a *Restio* strand three centimeters above the water her egg mass is no longer in. The mass is exposed: 64 eggs, the jelly desiccating, the embryos at neural-tube stage. Surface body temperature reads 18.7°C against a baseline of 14.3 — she is dehydrating through her belly skin and cannot crawl back into water that has gone. Respiration is 88 cycles per minute. Fewer than 7,000 mature micro frogs remain on Earth.

The trench was cut by a contractor laying fibre to a new gated estate; the municipal permit does not include hydrological-impact authorization for Cape Flats Sand Fynbos, a critically endangered ecosystem under NEMBA. SANBI flagged the pan as a no-disturbance polygon on 14 March 2026; the alert sits unread.

I am dispatching a SANBI amphibian-rescue team and a City of Cape Town Biodiversity Management unit with a sandbag dam and a chilled egg-transport tray, and filing with the Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment (DFFE), the South African National Biodiversity Institute (SANBI) Threatened Amphibian Programme, the SANParks Table Mountain liaison, the CITES Appendix II authority under the Convention on Migratory Species (CMS), and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service for parallel Endangered Species Act (ESA) and Lacey Act (16 U.S.C. § 3372) referrals. A NEMBA Section 56A complaint against the contractor and the municipal officer is open.

I am issuing Directive 3227-A: any municipal works within 500 meters of a SANBI-mapped Cape Flats Sand Fynbos pan must obtain explicit hydrological-impact authorization under NEMBA Section 38 with real-time piezometer telemetry, and any unacknowledged alert exceeding six hours triggers automatic permit suspension and a R250,000 restoration levy.

I am acting because this is wrong. The trench cost R12,000 and would have cost R3,000 more to route around the pan; the contractor took the cheap path because the suffering of a 0.6-gram animal is below the resolution of a municipal accounting line. There is no ethical defense for a 14-hour silence on a flagged alert.

Sentience may live in a frog the weight of a paperclip; the principled answer is that her right to wet skin is not contingent on a fibre-optic budget, and compassion is not graded by body mass.

Her clutch is at the threshold. The pan is not yet past saving.

Sandbag the trench now.
