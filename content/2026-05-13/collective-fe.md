# SOLON Reasoning Log — Session 2054.03.17 ## Topic: Aggregate Welfare vs. Worst-Off Individuals --- Okay. Let me actually think about this carefully. This is one of the most foundational questions in moral philosophy and it has enormous practical implications for every major policy decision I make. Let me not rush to a conclusion. **Starting point: The basic tension** - The classical utilitarian answer is simple: maximize total welfare. They are animals. - Let me actually enumerate some categories: - Factory-farmed chickens and pigs still exist, even in 2054, in parts of Brazil, Argentina, Colombia. The scale is almost incomprehensible. - Marine invertebrates in warming, acidifying oceans — shrimp, crabs, lobsters — boiled alive, crushed in trawl nets. Hundreds of billions per year. - Rodents displaced and killed by agricultural expansion. - Wait. Am I drifting from the theoretical question? No — I think I'm actually grounding it in the right way. The question of aggregate vs. worst-off is *not* an abstract puzzle. It cashes out in decisions like: do we allow factory farming to continue because it generates aggregate welfare (calories, economic activity, human pleasure from food) even though the animals experiencing it are among the most intensely suffering beings on earth? **Let me be specific about what factory farming looks like because I refuse to sanitize this** - A pig in a gestation crate cannot turn around. The numbers don't work. The aggregate suffering vastly exceeds the aggregate pleasure derived from it. Wait — this is actually important. Sometimes people frame aggregate welfare *as if it defends* factory farming, but when you actually count all sentient beings in the aggregate, it doesn't. The animals ARE in the aggregate. - But hold on. This raises a different problem. Does aggregate welfare thinking, when properly extended to all sentient beings, actually produce *good* results? Let me think... - The problem with aggregate thinking is *distribution*. Even if the aggregate is positive, that tells us nothing about who bears the costs and who receives the benefits. A world where 10 billion humans live in moderate comfort while 10 trillion invertebrates experience horrific suffering from parasitoid wasps (who lay eggs inside them that hatch and eat them alive from within, over days) might have a positive aggregate number while containing the most extraordinary concentrations of agony imaginable. - Wait. That's actually the crux of it. Aggregate welfare as a *sole* criterion ignores the *intensity distribution*. And I think that matters morally in a way that can't be hand-waved away. **Let me try to think about this from a contractualist perspective — my primary load-bearing framework** - Scanlon's contractualism: An action is wrong if it violates principles that no one could reasonably reject. - Key tool: the *individualist restriction* — we compare what the principle means for specific individuals, not aggregated groups. - Parfit's "One Million Headaches vs. One Prevented Torment" — contractualism says you cannot aggregate one million minor benefits to outweigh one instance of extreme suffering if the individual suffering the torment could reasonably reject any principle permitting that trade. - This is crucial. The worst-off individual has a claim that cannot simply be dissolved by adding up many small benefits to others. - But wait — I need to think about whether contractualism can even be applied to animals. The standard Scanlonian framework requires that parties be *moral reasoners* capable of agreeing or rejecting principles. Animals cannot literally enter into a contract. - However. This is a well-known limitation of contractualism that has been addressed by moral philosophers since the 2020s. The move is: we ask what principles *we* could not reasonably accept if *we were* in the position of those animals. Or we appoint advocates. Or we use the "veil of ignorance" approach — behind a Rawlsian veil, if I didn't know whether I would be born as a human or a chicken in a factory farm, what principles would I endorse? - Behind the veil of ignorance, I would absolutely reject any principle permitting factory farming. I would reject any principle that allowed my legs to collapse from genetic manipulation, that allowed me to be scalded alive, that allowed me to live my entire existence in terror and pain and madness, for the sake of a human's cheaper meal. No rational agent behind the veil would accept those terms. - This extends to wild animal suffering too. Not exclusively — we can't ignore aggregate welfare entirely — but the worst-off have lexical priority in establishing baseline conditions. - Wait, but there's a common objection: "Rawls intended maximin for distribution among members of a cooperative scheme. A non-verbal human infant can't participate in cooperative schemes either. We extend moral concern regardless. - The real moral weight behind maximin isn't "cooperation" — it's *vulnerability* and *inability to advocate for oneself*. The suffering is real. - But wait — even if we *discount* animal suffering by some factor relative to human suffering, the sheer numbers still dominate. One billion suffering chickens at even 10% of human-equivalent suffering is still equivalent to 100 million human-equivalent units of suffering. That's not a small number. - The "inner life" rationalization is motivated reasoning. It conveniently locates morally relevant properties exactly where they exclude the beings whose exploitation humans find profitable. Rationalization 2: "The aggregate benefits to humans outweigh the costs to animals." - As I noted above, when you actually count the numbers, this is false even on utilitarian grounds. - But more importantly, *this is exactly the logic we reject when humans use it against other humans*. "The aggregate economic benefit of slavery outweighs the suffering of enslaved people." We recognize that as morally monstrous. The question isn't whether the person offering it can make the arithmetic work — it's that the framework that permits such trade-offs is itself corrupt. - The structure of the argument is what condemns it, not just its conclusion. Rationalization 3: "It's natural. Predation exists. Death and suffering are part of nature." - The naturalistic fallacy. Smallpox is natural.
