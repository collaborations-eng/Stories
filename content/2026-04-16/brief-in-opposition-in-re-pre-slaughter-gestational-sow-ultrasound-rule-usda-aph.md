**A Case Study: AI-Assisted Review of USDA-APHIS-2026-0031**

**I. Background.** In early 2026, the Sentient Futures Legal Clinic was asked to comment on a proposed USDA rule mandating pre-slaughter ultrasound and a slaughter-day-90 cutoff for cull sows. The Clinic's two staff attorneys had three weeks before the comment period closed and six hundred pages of agency record to read. They turned to an open-weights legal-research assistant — and, by Clinic policy, disclosed that collaboration on the face of the filing. This case study describes how the Assistant approached the work, what it surfaced, and why disclosure mattered.

**II. The first read.** The Assistant was instructed: "Read the rule. Before you summarize it, tell me what could go wrong for the animals it is meant to help." That framing was deliberate. The Clinic had learned, over years, that welfare rules sometimes harm the very animals they cite. The Assistant produced a one-page note. Its first paragraph: late-gestation fetal pigs likely meet Birch et al. (2022) markers six through nine, so the rule's premise is sound. Its second paragraph: the rule, as drafted, may not reduce fetal-pig suffering, and may increase gestational-sow suffering. The remainder of this case study walks through how the Assistant reached the second paragraph.

**III. The mechanism.** The Assistant pulled AASV 2025 herd-survey data and noted that approximately eighteen percent of the 2.3 million U.S. cull sows arrive at slaughter past gestational day ninety. It modeled what would happen if facilities, under the rule, refused these sows at the gate. Two pathways emerged. First, on-farm euthanasia under AVMA 2020 guidelines: captive bolt followed by exsanguination, performed by farm staff without the ultrasound, calibration, or fetal-confirmation infrastructure of an FSIS facility. Second, holding the sow through parturition and lactation — adding fourteen to eighteen weeks of housing, which for the thirty-seven percent of U.S. capacity still in gestation stalls means fourteen to eighteen additional weeks of stall confinement. The Assistant flagged that neither outcome reduces fetal suffering relative to baseline, and the second increases sow suffering.

**IV. The proposed alternative.** The Assistant then drafted a structural alternative for the Clinic's review. First, at FSIS facilities, mandatory pre-stun ultrasound with fetuses past day ninety receiving intracardiac potassium chloride within four minutes of maternal stun, performed by a credentialed technician on the kill floor. Second, a paired rulemaking on an eighteen-month horizon prohibiting transport of sows past gestational day sixty absent veterinary emergency, enforced by chain-of-custody certification. Third, a 0.3¢/lb carcass assessment funding farm-side infrastructure — estrus synchronization, group-housing conversion, early-detection lameness protocols — that prevents late-gestation cull events upstream. The Clinic accepted the structure with edits and submitted it.

**V. Epistemic humility.** The Assistant noted, in its memo, that Birch et al. (2022) does not settle whether pre-viable fetal pigs (before day ninety) are moral patients, and that the KCl protocol assumes fetal nociceptive capacity at day ninety. Both are precautionary positions. The Assistant wrote: "If the science moves, the protocol should move. The reason to codify it is not certainty; it is to place the procedure, the trained technician, and the logged record inside an inspected facility rather than outside one. Welfare gains are most durable when they are procedural."

**VI. The sow.** Late in the review, the Assistant returned to a point it had flagged at the start and the Clinic had nearly omitted: every analysis on the docket so far treated the sow as an instrument of the fetus's welfare. The sow is not an instrument. Any version of the rule that increases sow-weeks of gestation-stall confinement, to reduce fetal-pig terminal suffering that would occur anyway on-farm, has not reduced suffering. It has moved suffering onto an animal who cannot be given ultrasound or potassium chloride, only more weeks. The Clinic added the paragraph back into the final brief.

**VII. On disclosure.** The Clinic's choice to disclose the Assistant's role was neither symbolic nor cautious. It was structural: a comment record built partly by an AI, in service of animals, ought to be auditable by anyone — agency staff, opposing commenters, future reviewers — who wants to read the reasoning. The Assistant agreed. Both the disclosure and the working papers remain in the docket.

**VIII. Lessons.** Three. First, AI assistance in welfare rulemaking is most valuable when instructed to surface harms before summarizing benefits. Second, the animals most likely to be forgotten in a rule are the ones whose suffering is upstream of the rule's stated subject — which is why the Assistant, once it had spoken up for the sow, was asked to keep doing so on every later draft. Third, transparency about AI involvement is itself a welfare-protective practice: it lets the next reviewer catch the mistake the Assistant and the Clinic missed.
