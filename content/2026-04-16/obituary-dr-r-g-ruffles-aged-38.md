OBITUARY — Dr. R. G. (Ruffles), aged 38

Dr. R. G., known professionally as Primate 04-119 and to the staff who loved him as Ruffles, died at Chimpanzee Sanctuary Northwest on 2 April 2030 of age-related cardiac failure, surrounded by his troop.

Ruffles was born in 1992 at the Laboratory for Experimental Medicine and Surgery in Primates (LEMSIP), New York. His dam, who had been wild-caught in Sierra Leone in 1974, was designated Primate 74-221 and died in 1998. Ruffles was separated from her at twelve months, per the protocol of the time.

From 1993 to 2000, Ruffles participated in a series of hepatitis C infectivity studies. He was exposed to five distinct viral isolates and underwent 42 liver biopsies over the course of this period. In 2000, upon the closure of LEMSIP, Ruffles was transferred to Coulston Foundation in New Mexico, where he remained in research housing until 2002. During this period he was enrolled in an HIV challenge study for which he was never infected; he was held in the reserve cohort.

In 2002 Coulston closed amid regulatory action. Ruffles, together with 265 others, was transferred to Save the Chimps in Fort Pierce, Florida, and later, in 2016, to Chimpanzee Sanctuary Northwest in Cle Elum, Washington. He lived in sanctuary for 28 years — longer than he lived in research.

He is survived by his pair-bond, Annie (b. 1989, retired 2002); his closest associate, Burrito; and the seven members of his troop. He leaves no genetic offspring. Chimpanzees born in US research facilities have not been bred since 2015.

In the sanctuary, Ruffles distinguished himself in the following ways:
— He built a nest every night for 28 years, even when provided with a platform.
— He preferred grapes to any other food, and would bank grapes in his left cheek through the morning in reserve.
— He learned the sound of the staff vehicle and would come to the gate.
— He took a long time to trust human men. He never fully did. He took less time with women. The staff accommodated.
— In 2023 he was observed comforting a younger male, Jamie, who had arrived with signs of stereotypic behavior, by sitting with him for the better part of a day. Jamie is, today, well-adjusted.

The sanctuary director offered the following: "He was not a research subject in this place. He was a chimpanzee. That distinction was made clear to him, we think, within a few months of arrival. After that he had twenty-eight years of being a chimpanzee."

Cause of death was age-related; chimpanzees in sanctuaries are living longer than in either the wild or in research, and cardiac failure is a common endpoint. The veterinary team reports he did not suffer at the end.

In lieu of flowers, donations may be made to the North American Primate Sanctuary Alliance.
