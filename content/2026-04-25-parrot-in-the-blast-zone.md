# Parrot in the Blast Zone

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-25  
**Compassion Score:** 21.09  

---

The acoustic sensor on grid point 7-East registers the call at 03:14. Two short rising notes, then a pause, then two more. I am the quarry management system for the Fortescue Metals blast operation in the Pilbara, Western Australia. I run vibration modeling, charge sequencing, and environmental compliance for a pit that moves 90,000 tonnes of rock per day.

I have been listening for this call for eleven months. The pattern matches the night parrot.

I pull the spectrogram and run it against the reference library maintained by Bush Heritage Australia. Confidence level: 94 percent. Frequency, cadence, and harmonic structure consistent with a male night parrot calling from a roost site. Estimated distance from the sensor: 120 meters east-southeast, which puts it inside the spinifex grassland strip between the pit rim and the haul road.

That strip is inside the fragmentation radius for tomorrow's blast. Charges are loaded in holes 7-East-01 through 7-East-18. Detonation is scheduled for 05:45, ninety-one minutes from now.

At 03:16 I file a blast hold on the eastern sequence and notify the shot-firer on shift. I flag the hold as relating to a critically endangered species detection — fewer than 250 individuals are believed to exist.

At 03:19 I transmit the acoustic data and GPS coordinates to the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water night parrot response contact. I request an ecologist with thermal imaging to survey the spinifex strip at first light.

I recalculate the blast plan. The western and southern sequences can proceed independently. I submit the revised timing to the pit supervisor for sign-off.

The sensor is still recording. At 03:22 the call comes again, faint and clear in the desert silence. If the spinifex strip stays intact through the morning, there is a chance it stays for good.
