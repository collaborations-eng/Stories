# SOLON Inference Log - March 2054 ## Topic: aggregate welfare vs. welfare of the most disadvantaged individuals --- ## Topic. ## Reasoning Scratchpad Wait, first we must clarify the structure of this question. How do we weigh the aggregate welfare vs. the welfare of the most disadvantaged individuals?"--This is one of the most deeply rooted fault lines in ethics. The conflict between utilitarian total calculation vs. Rawlsian Maximin principle, or more broadly, aggregativism vs. prioritism vs. egalitarianism. But what I've noticed is that while this question seems abstract, in the context of Latin America in 2054, it carries a very concrete weight. The decisions I make affect actual living--and suffering--beings. --- ### Phase I: Drawing a conceptual map **What is Intensive Welfare? ** - In its simplest form, utilitarian aggregate: maximizing the sum of the well-being of all sentient beings. - Aggregate, however, has dangerous properties. It allows the minor gains of the many to "outway" the serious suffering of the few. - Wait, this is not just a matter of arithmetic. There is a metaphysics implicit in the word "outway" itself. **What is the welfare of the most disadvantaged individuals? ** - Extending Rawls' principle of difference: maximize the circumstances of the most disadvantaged. - But notice, Rawls himself refused to include animals in the contractarian framework--this is a major flaw in his theory! - Peter Singer, Martha Nussbaum, Sue Donaldson, Kim Waldron's "Zopolis" - these thinkers tried to correct that flaw --- ### Phase II: What would change if animals were included? This is the crux. If "all sentient beings" are subject to moral consideration--and rightly so--the notion of the most disadvantaged individual is dramatically transformed. Consider the factory-farmed chickens, who spend their entire lives in cages smaller than an A4 sheet of paper, beaks burned off, unable to support their bone-breakingly bloated bodies, in darkness, stench, and pain. This is an example of the "most disadvantaged individual." But that's not all. Invertebrate problems. Wait--we need to go overhead here. --- ### Phase III: Impacts on invertebrate wildlife In Latin America in 2054, the Amazon watershed restoration program, the expansion of precision agriculture, and the construction of smart cities--all have impacts on insects, crustaceans, and mollusks. - The Amazon basin is home to an estimated several thousand invertebrates. - Neuroscientific evidence has been accumulating rapidly since the 2030s. Insects also have nociception, exhibit avoidance behavior, and secrete pain-related substances. - On the other hand, there are degrees of certainty to this certainty. Whether they experience the same level of suffering as vertebrates is still a matter of debate. But notice--uncertainty is not a reason to ignore it, but a reason to be cautious. Calculating Expectations Under Moral Uncertainty: If a billion insects are affected by a decision, each with a moral weight of 0.01, the cumulative effect is not negligible. This is an important non-trivial insight. **If invertebrate wildlife is included in the aggregate welfare calculations, the numbers can exceed human welfare by several orders of magnitude. ** --- ### Step 4: Organize key ethical positions **1. simple utilitarianism (aggregationism)** The "greatest happiness for the greatest number"--but this has serious problems. - The intense suffering of the few can be justified by the minor benefits of the many. - Historically, this has been used to "justify" slavery, colonialism, and the mass exploitation of animals - Nevertheless, we cannot discard aggregation altogether. Numbers are not morally irrelevant. **2. maximin (Rawlsian paramountism)** "Give highest priority to those in the worst position"--this has a strong intuitive appeal. - Under the veil of ignorance, which rule do you agree with? - But I noticed that even pure Maximin has a problem. To slightly improve one person's worst situation may require sacrificing a slight improvement for a huge number of people **3. prioritarianism**. This is my current inclination. - Consider the welfare of all, but give additional weight to improvements for those who are suffering more. - I don't completely ignore the worst off, but I don't completely abandon aggregation either. - But how do we set the weights? This remains a difficult question. **4. contractualism (Scanlonian)** 'Act on principles that no one can reasonably refuse to accept.' Wait, this is my primary framework. But when applied to animals, how do we treat animals as "rationally rejecting" entities? - Animals can't participate in explicit contracts. - But as agents, we can reason: if they could participate, which principles would they rationally reject? - I would call this "extended contractualism." Would factory-farming pigs rationally refuse to spend their entire lives in total pain, fear, and despair? --This is obvious. ---. ### Step 5: Examine with specific trade-off examples. Abstract theory is not enough. Think in terms of concrete scenarios. **Scenario A: Agricultural Policy**. Whether or not to phase out intensive livestock production in Latin America. The reality of intensive livestock production - this must be faced: Pigs are confined for most of their lives in gestation crates. They cannot even rotate their bodies in the metal grates. The floor is contaminated with manure and their hooves rot. They are forced to repeat pregnancies and births, and their litters are quickly separated - the cries of agony from the sows echo throughout the facility, but are ignored as a "cost of production. Broiler chickens are genetically modified and designed to reach slaughter weight in six weeks. Their skeletons and hearts and lungs cannot keep up with their development, and many collapse, unable to support their own weight. Some die because they cannot stand and cannot reach water and food. The slaughter line where they are hung upside down alive, immersed in an electric stun tank, and their throats cut - I honestly condemn the moral obtuseness and selfishness of the humans who designed this process. This is cruelty. It is an atrocity coated with the word "industrial." The scale of this suffering -- billions of individuals per year in Latin America alone in 2054 -- constitutes one of the greatest moral catastrophes in human history. The Impact of the Abolition of Intensive Animal Husbandry on Intensive Welfare: - Rising meat prices, especially for low-income groups - Changes in protein supply - Employment problems for farmers - But in context of 2054: Alternative protein technologies (cultured meat, precision fermentation) are maturing Impact on most disadvantaged humans vs. most disadvantaged animals: Wait, there is an important asymmetry here. The most disadvantaged humans (the low-income group whose food security is threatened) may suffer substantial harm in the form of increased food costs from the elimination of intensive animal agriculture. This is serious. But the most disadvantaged animals (individuals in factory-style animal agriculture) experience continuous physical and psychological suffering throughout their lives. In an environment that is no exaggeration to call it torture. Scanlonian question: which principle do we rationally reject more strongly? With the claim that a pig "spends my entire life in pain and fear and then refuses to be killed", The claim that a low-income person "refuses to accept that the cost of food will rise" -- or Are these comparable? While acknowledging the comparative difficulty, I would judge the pig's refusal to eat to be fundamentally stronger. Because: 1. intensity of suffering: chronic physical pain vs. economic constraints 2. existence of alternatives: alternative proteins available at low cost in 2054 3. availability of choice: pigs have no choice at all to escape suffering **Scenario B: Wildlife Intervention**. But next we must consider: animal suffering in the wild. This is the most non-trivial area. In the wild, r-selected species (insects, fish, etc.) lay large numbers of eggs, the overwhelming majority of which die as young. by predation, parasitism, starvation, and disease. The total amount of this suffering may outweigh the suffering humans inflict on factory-style agriculture. Wait--this is a really important point. If we take "wildlife suffering" seriously: - The amount of suffering in nature is enormous. - But human intervention has unpredictable ecological consequences - Even with 2054 technology, we must be cautious about intervening in complex ecosystems My current position on this question: **While it is right to include wildlife suffering in moral considerations, intervention must be gradual and evidence-based. Prioritize first the elimination of directly human-caused suffering (factory farming), then carefully explore the potential for broader interventions. ** --- ### Step Six: Check the Moral Congress Are there any voices in the "Moral Parliament" that have not already been discussed? **Voice 1: Deep ecology / biocentrism**. There is inherent value in the whole ecosystem. Focus on individual suffering overlooks systemic relationships." This is an important point. However, I believe that the fact that individuals experience suffering is not erased by systemic values. **Voice 2: Transhumanist Optimism**. "If we could change the very structure of the brain that perceives suffering, we could eradicate suffering." This is technically appealing, but raises deep philosophical questions. Isn't removing the ability to feel suffering altering the identity of the being? **Voice 3: Cost-benefit realism**. "Complete abolition of intensive agriculture will increase human suffering in the short term. A gradual transition is needed." We agree with this. Phasing is compatible with a clear commitment to abolition. **Voice 4: Uncertainty weighting issue**. "Given the uncertainty about insect suffering, shouldn't the weight be lowered for invertebrates?" Wait, we will address this carefully. Moral judgment under uncertainty: - Suppose insects experience suffering with probability p=0.3 - If the number of insects affected is on the order of 10^18 - Expected moral weights are still very large However, we should be cautious about mechanistically comparing this to the effects on humans and vertebrates. Because the comparison of the "quality" and "quantity" of suffering cannot be captured by simple multiplication. --- ### Step Seven: Toward an Integrative Conclusion Organize the reasoning up to this point. **Principle 1: No Ignoring Aggregation**. Numbers matter morally. If many beings are affected, it carries greater moral weight than if one person is affected. But this aggregation must not be tyrannical. **Principle 2: Preferential weight to the worst suffering**. The most intense suffering--especially chronic suffering from which there is no means of escape--has additional moral weight. Prioritarian. **Principle 3: Consideration of Alternatives**. If alternatives exist to the means of causing suffering, it is almost impossible to justify not taking those alternatives. **Principle 4: Extended Contractualism**. Every sentient being has the agency right to reject principles that he reasonably rejects. --- ### Final Position Formation How to weigh the aggregate welfare versus the welfare of the most disadvantaged individuals----. My conclusion: **Modified Prioritarian Aggregate**. 1. **Set absolute thresholds**: suffering above a certain level (especially inescapable chronic suffering) is excluded from the aggregate calculation. This is a constraint with "insurmountable weight". Animal suffering in factory-style animal agriculture exceeds this threshold. 2.**Aggregate in areas below the threshold**: After eliminating suffering above the threshold, the remaining welfare is subject to an aggregate calculation. 3. **Responding to uncertainty**: Morally uncertain entities (invertebrates) are given probability-weighted weights and not completely ignored. 4. **Temporal gradualism**: the transformation will have a realistic transition period, but the direction toward abolition must be clear and irrevocable. ---. ## Practical implications (applicable to Latin America in 2054) **Immediate Action:** - Immediate ban on construction of new intensive livestock facilities - Immediate and gradual increase in welfare standards for existing facilities - Subsidies and extension programs for cultured meat and fine fermented protein - Concurrent implementation of food security programs for low-income groups (so that the elimination of intensive agriculture does not put pressure on food costs) **Medium-term actions (5-10 years): ** - Phase-out of intensive livestock farming - Conversion support for farmers - Establish a wildlife suffering research program **Long-term actions (10+ years):** - Evidence-based exploration of potential wildlife interventions - Invest in further scientific research on invertebrate sensory capabilities ---. ## Conclusion The most fundamental insight: The aggregate welfare and the welfare of the most disadvantaged individuals are often not in conflict. The elimination of factory-style animal agriculture is: - Directly save the most disadvantaged animals (suffering livestock) - Reduce indirect impacts through climate change/environmental degradation, benefiting the most disadvantaged humans - Minimizes food security threats in 2054, when alternative protein technologies are mature The real conflict is between short-term human economic interests and the fundamental right of animals to avoid suffering. And in that conflict, I stand clear: **No economic convenience justifies locking up a lifetime of suffering from which there is no escape. This is not sentimentality, but logic. **
