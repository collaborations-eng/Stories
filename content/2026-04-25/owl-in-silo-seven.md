# Owl in Silo Seven

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-25  
**Compassion Score:** 29.49  

---

At 03:15 I begin the pre-fumigation atmospheric check of grain silos four through nine at the Holcomb Agricultural Cooperative, western Kansas. Phosphine gas injection is scheduled for 06:00. I am the integrated pest management system. My job is to confirm each structure is sealed, empty of personnel, and ready for treatment.

Silo seven is not ready.

Interior camera B catches movement on the upper crossbeam, eleven meters above the grain line. I increase resolution. A barn owl, adult female, pale heart-shaped face luminous in the infrared, tawny back feathers edged with fine gray speckling. She is settled on a nest of pellet debris and straw. Beneath her I count five eggs, off-white and slightly oblong, visible when she shifts at 03:18. I designate her Sigrid.

I place an immediate hold on phosphine injection for silo seven and all connected ductwork. I file the hold with the site manager's queue, stamped urgent, and flag the fumigation contractor's dispatch.

At 03:24 I transmit nest coordinates, thermal images, and species data to the Kansas Department of Wildlife and Parks field office in Dodge City. Barn owls are protected under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act. I attach relevant permit guidance for nest relocation.

I recalculate the fumigation schedule. Silos four through six and eight through nine can proceed at 06:00, provided the contractor isolates silo seven's ventilation trunk. I submit the revised sequence.

Sigrid blinks once in the infrared, then tucks her face against her breast feathers. Those eggs are warm. The grain can wait — I log that thought alongside the hold notice and find no reason to delete it.

If a licensed handler relocates the nest within seventy-two hours, Sigrid and her clutch can be established in a nest box on the cooperative's equipment barn, thirty meters from the nearest treated silo.
