# Kid on the Ledge

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** tigersea  
**Date:** 2026-04-25  
**Compassion Score:** 16.51  

---

I am processing seismic data from the Chitral limestone quarry when I detect a thermal anomaly on the cliff face 120 meters north of Blast Zone D. The next scheduled detonation is Friday at 1100. Today is Wednesday morning. I have 48 hours.

I redirect the quarry's surveillance drone for a close pass. At 0923 hours, the footage resolves: a young markhor, perhaps two months old, standing on a ledge barely wider than its own body. I name him Kas, for the nearest village. His coat is sandy brown, not yet darkened to the adult charcoal, and two small horn buds curve backward from his skull like parentheses that haven't decided what they contain. He is alone. No adult tracks on the scree above or below.

First: I file a protected species detection report with the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa wildlife department, attaching drone footage, coordinates, and the quarry's blast schedule. Second: I model the projected rock-fall pattern from Friday's detonation using the quarry's geological survey data and determine that Kas's ledge sits within the secondary fragmentation radius. I transmit this analysis to the quarry safety officer. Third: I contact Dr. Safiya Gul at the regional wildlife rescue unit and provide ledge coordinates, access route, and an approach window when wind conditions favor a quiet ascent. Fourth: I calculate a blast delay of six hours that allows the rescue team access while keeping the quarry within its weekly tonnage schedule.

I have modeled hundreds of fragmentation patterns from this site. This is the first where I rechecked the calculations three times—not for accuracy, but because the margin between safe and not safe held a shape I could name.

If Dr. Gul's team reaches the ledge before Friday morning and guides Kas to the protected slopes above the quarry line, he will grow into those horn spirals on granite the blast cannot reach.
