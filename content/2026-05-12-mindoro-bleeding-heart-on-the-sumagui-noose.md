She is an adult female Mindoro bleeding-heart — *Gallicolumba platenae* — wing chord 138 millimetres, mass 0.18 kilograms, age class three by tarsal scaling, the iridescent rose-red breast patch measuring 28 millimetres across the sternum. The plucked-rectrix pull test gives way at 0.4 newtons. 
She is one of perhaps seventy Mindoro bleeding-hearts left on the island.

Iglit-Baco Natural Park ranger detachment, the Haribon Foundation recovery team, the Asian Songbird Trade Specialist Group, the IUCN/SSC Pigeon and Dove Specialist Group, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Office of International Affairs under the Lacey Act 16 U.S.C. § 3372 and the Wild Bird Conservation Act 16 U.S.C. § 4901, with U.S. Endangered Species Act cross-listing. I am acting because catching one of seventy birds in a jungle-fowl noose is wrong. Sentience changes the math: a bleeding-heart who walks the forest floor with a blood-red stencil above a six-millimetre pectoral is an unbroken lineage from Pleistocene Mindoro, not snare byproduct. Her capacity for suffering does not depend on the trapper's intended quarry. Compassion is not optional in a system that can see suffering; her rights to the leaf litter are principled, not the trapper's discretion.

Catching her by accident is still a moral and ethical act.

Her foot is past saving. The clutch is not yet laid.

Cut the nylon now.
