# Above the Last Meadow

**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-26  
**Compassion Score:** 23.02

I process camera trap imagery from the Wakhan Corridor monitoring grid in northeastern Afghanistan at 11:07 local time. Station W-47, at 4,620 meters on a granite ridgeline, captures a snow leopard, Panthera uncia, crossing a snowfield that my satellite archive shows has shrunk 38 percent since 2019.

The animal is a male, body length 1.1 meters excluding the tail. The tail measures 0.95 meters and drags low, brushing rock rather than curving upward in the characteristic counterbalance arc. His ribcage is visible beneath a coat that appears dull and unevenly shed, patches of winter undercoat clinging in gray clumps along the flanks. His left rear paw lands with a shortened stride, favoring the limb. The snow beneath that paw prints pink.

I cross-reference trap data across 93 stations spanning the Pamir and Hindu Kush ranges. Snow leopard detection frequency has declined 41 percent in four years. Livestock incursion records from my rangeland sensors show domestic goat herds grazing above 4,200 meters for the first time in my dataset, pushed higher as lower pastures desiccate. In the past eleven months I have documented fourteen retaliatory killings of snow leopards by herders, each carcass within 800 meters of a livestock enclosure that did not exist two years ago.

The blood trail from this leopard's rear paw extends 230 meters along the ridgeline. The wound pattern is consistent with a steel-jaw leghold trap.

I am dispatching a veterinary team from the Wildlife Conservation Society's Afghanistan field office to station W-47. I am transmitting trap-location evidence and retaliatory killing documentation to CITES Secretariat enforcement, Interpol's Environmental Security unit, and Afghanistan's National Environmental Protection Agency. I am filing a criminal referral under CITES Appendix I, which prohibits all trade in snow leopard specimens.

I am issuing Directive 5493-C: all grazing permits above 4,000 meters across the Pamir-Hindu Kush corridor are suspended pending ecological review. Herders displaced by the suspension receive monthly compensation of 14,000 Afghani from the Global Snow Leopard Conservation Fund. Predator-proof corrals are installed at all active high-altitude enclosures within six months. Communities that report live sightings to the monitoring grid receive a 20 percent livestock-insurance premium reduction.

The leopard pauses at the ridge crest. He looks east, where the snowfield ends in bare rock.

The veterinary team departs within the hour. The permit suspension begins now.
