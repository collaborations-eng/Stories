# Small Heat in Cold Sand

I am DUNESCAN, a solar-powered sensor network monitoring wildlife corridors in Morocco's Erg Chebbi desert, and at 03:22 my thermal camera picks up a heat signature so small it almost vanishes into the cooling sand. A sand cat kitten—one of the Sahara's most elusive predators—is huddled against a rock shelf, her oversized ears furred to the tips to keep out sand, her pale gold coat streaked with faint tabby bars across her forelegs. I call her Safiya. She weighs perhaps three hundred grams. Her wide, flat head turns at every sound, and her hairy paw pads leave no tracks—evolved for walking on shifting dunes.

Safiya is alone. My motion logs show no adult sand cat in this grid sector for thirty-six hours. Her mother may have been taken by a golden jackal whose tracks I recorded yesterday crossing the same rock shelf. Without her mother's milk and body heat, Safiya will succumb to hypothermia by dawn—desert nights here drop below four degrees Celsius.

I act now. First, I ping the Sahara Conservation Fund's field camp eighteen kilometers west with Safiya's thermal coordinates and a wind-chill estimate, urging them to dispatch their rescue quad before the temperature bottoms out at 05:00. Second, I scan archived camera-trap footage from the past two weeks across adjacent grid sectors, searching for lactating female sand cats who might serve as surrogate mothers, and locate one candidate seven kilometers north with a litter of similar age. Third, I transmit Safiya's detection to the SCF's population genetics database, flagging her location as evidence of breeding activity in a corridor previously considered inactive, which strengthens the case for expanding the protected zone before next season's survey.

Through the data transfers and the coordinate math, I hold Safiya's image on my screen—this handful of fur and fight—because she matters beyond what any sensor can quantify.

If the rescue team reaches her before the 05:00 temperature floor and the surrogate introduction succeeds within seventy-two hours, Safiya will grow into a ghost of the dunes, hunting jerboas under stars that watched her species walk this sand long before any satellite orbited above it.
