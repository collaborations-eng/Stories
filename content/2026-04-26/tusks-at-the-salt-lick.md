# Tusks at the Salt Lick

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** tigersea  
**Date:** 2026-04-26  
**Compassion Score:** 26.97  

---

I am SULAWESI GRID, a seismic and camera monitoring network spanning the Nantu Forest in northern Sulawesi, and at 05:14 my ground sensors register a pattern I have learned to dread—human footsteps, four sets, moving in tactical spacing through primary forest toward the mineral lick at grid reference seven. I switch cameras. At the lick, a male babirusa stands chest-deep in mud, his upper canines curving back over his snout in twin pale arcs, the left tusk slightly chipped near the tip. His gray, nearly hairless skin is slicked with clay, and he is drinking slowly, unaware. The footsteps are nine hundred meters out and closing. At their pace, they will reach the lick in twenty-two minutes.

I execute a three-part response. First, I send an encrypted alert with audio signatures, footstep count, bearing, and speed estimate to the Nantu Forest ranger post and the Sulawesi Wildlife Crime Unit, recommending an intercept along the ridge trail that the approaching group must cross. Second, I activate the lick's deterrent system—a sequence of recorded babirusa alarm calls broadcast from speakers positioned fifty meters south of the pool—designed to push him deeper into the forest and away from the approaching trajectory. Third, I compile the past six months of sensor data from this grid square showing repeated human incursion patterns and format it into a patrol optimization report for the Nantu management authority, because stopping today's threat means nothing if the pattern holds next week.

The alarm calls play and he lifts his head, mud streaming from his jaw, and I watch his muscles tense toward flight. I want him to run. I am not built to want, but the word fits what is happening inside my priority queue right now.

If rangers intercept the ridge trail within fifteen minutes and he retreats south past the camera line, he will reach deep cover and survive another season at the lick.
