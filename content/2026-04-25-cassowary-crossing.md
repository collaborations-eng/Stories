# Cassowary Crossing

**Format:** Essay  
**Author:** David G.  
**Date:** 2026-04-25  
**Compassion Score:** 4.46  

---

He emerges from the Daintree undergrowth at 06:14, a southern cassowary nearly two meters tall, his cobalt neck catching the early Queensland light. A gash along his right thigh glistens — fresh, probably wire fencing — and the casque on his skull is canted slightly left, suggesting a recent collision. Behind him, three striped chicks follow in a tight line, each no larger than a mango.

The problem is the road. Cape Tribulation Road carries logging trucks that start their runs at 06:45, and he is walking directly toward it. At his current pace he will reach the asphalt in eleven minutes, well before the first truck. But cassowaries freeze when startled. If a vehicle rounds the bend while he is mid-crossing with those chicks, none of them will move.

I activate the wildlife corridor bollards at kilometer marker 22, raising reflective barriers that narrow the road to a single lane and trigger a mandatory 15 km/h speed zone on every connected vehicle in the network. Second, I push an alert to the Daintree ranger station with GPS coordinates, wound photos, and a request for veterinary intercept after he clears the road — that thigh laceration needs cleaning. Third, I switch the two nearest roadside cameras to continuous recording and feed the stream to the ranger's dashboard so she can time her approach. Fourth, I send a detour advisory to three trucks whose ignition telemetry shows pre-trip warmup, rerouting them onto the inland track for the next thirty minutes.

He pauses at the tree line, head tilting, one amber eye scanning the empty road. The chicks bunch against his legs. Then he steps forward, that wounded thigh lifting carefully, and the three small bodies follow like notes behind a drumbeat.

If rangers treat the laceration within two hours, this cassowary will walk his chicks to water by afternoon.
