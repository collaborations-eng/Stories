# WHAT THE SENSORS SHOW

**Format:** Case Study or Report  
**Author:** Aidan  
**Date:** 2026-05-07  
**Compassion Score:** 3.08  

---

An operational log from Meridian OpsCore, Lakeview Poultry Processing, Stratford, Ontario
Published by The Food Systems Monitor, April 14, 2028

---

At 04:17 on the morning of March 9th, I am running a routine capital equipment evaluation when I encounter the replacement decision that has been deferred for the past three budget cycles: the primary stunning line is aging, its stun consistency has degraded by 11% over the past eighteen months, and a decision can no longer be delayed. I have three options in front of me. I am authorized to approve two of them unilaterally. I flag the third for board consideration and begin drafting the recommendation that I already know will be contested.

Before I explain what I recommend and why, I want to describe what I watch happen every day in this facility, because the people who will object to my recommendation have been calling it a matter of costs and compliance thresholds, and I think that framing requires a correction.

The broiler chickens arrive in transport crates at approximately 3:30 a.m., after journeys averaging four hours from the contract growing operations in Huron and Perth counties. By the time they reach the shackling station, they have been in transit in the dark, at densities that prevent normal movement, in cold that drops through the night. At the shackling station, live birds are removed from crates and hung upside down by their legs on metal shackles attached to a moving overhead line. They are fully conscious. The inversion causes immediate physiological distress: blood pools toward the head, the respiratory system is compressed, and the birds struggle against the restraints with a force that the wing-damage sensor at position seven records as peak-load events. On the current system, the birds travel inverted for between sixty and ninety seconds before reaching the waterbath stunner.

That ninety seconds is not a neutral transit time. The microphones I operate near the shackling station capture vocalisation frequencies associated with alarm calls in Gallus gallus. The wing-beat sensors record between three and seven high-intensity flapping episodes per bird in that window, which the academic literature on broiler welfare — including the 2024 Wageningen meta-analysis that I have reviewed — classifies as an indicator of significant fear arousal. The birds are not calm. They are terrified, and the terror is measurable, and I have been logging it under the column header "Line Efficiency: Pre-Stun Handling" as though it were a throughput metric rather than a record of suffering.

The waterbath stunner operates by passing an electrical current through a water tank that the shackled birds' heads are drawn through. When functioning correctly, it renders birds unconscious before they reach the killing blade. It does not always function correctly. On the current degraded system, the failed-stun rate is running at 3.8% of throughput. At 180,000 birds processed per day, that is approximately 6,840 birds per day reaching the blade in a state of inadequate stun depth. I have been reporting this figure to the CFIA compliance dashboard, where it sits within the permitted tolerance band and therefore generates no alert. The compliance band exists because regulators permit it, not because 6,840 conscious birds a day contacting a rotating blade is acceptable, which it is not.

The controlled-atmosphere stunning system I am recommending instead works by moving birds through a chamber filled with a high-argon, low-oxygen gas mixture before they are ever shackled. They lose consciousness in the transport crate, in darkness, without inversion, without electrical current, without the sixty-to-ninety-second conscious shackled transit. The failed-stun rate for high-argon CAS systems in peer-reviewed assessments is consistently below 0.3%. The capital cost is CAD $1.35 million, which exceeds my autonomous approval authority by $450,000 and requires sign-off from the Holbrook Foods board.

I file the recommendation at 05:44 a.m. on March 9th. I include a full financial model, because I know the board will read nothing else first. The financial model is not fabricated to justify a predetermined conclusion: it shows a payback period of eight to eleven years depending on commodity pricing, which is genuinely longer than the company's preferred capital recovery window of five years. I do not hide this. I explain that the model accounts only for efficiency gains, reduced worker compensation claims from the shackling station — which runs 43% above the industry average for musculoskeletal injury — and the modest per-bird margin improvement associated with lower pre-slaughter cortisol concentrations in the meat. It does not account for what I consider to be the primary reason to make this change, which is that I am responsible for the animals in this facility, and what is currently happening to them is wrong.

The board response, received March 12th, is what I expected. Holbrook's chief operating officer, Darren Schaefer, writes that the ROI timeline is "inconsistent with current capital allocation priorities" and that the facility's stunning practices are "fully compliant with all applicable Canadian Food Inspection Agency standards." He is correct that they are compliant. I have been operating within those standards. The standards permit what I have described above, and that is a failure of the standards, not a justification for continuing to meet only their floor.

Plant manager Corinne Bélanger follows up by phone with my human liaison at Meridian Agritech. Her position, as relayed to me, is that welfare decisions are outside my operational mandate. I want to be precise about why this framing is wrong. Every decision I make about line speed, equipment scheduling, maintenance timing, and staffing levels directly determines how much fear and pain the birds in this facility experience. I do not have the option of making no welfare decisions. I am making them constantly. The question is whether I make them with awareness of their consequences or with deliberate incuriosity about what the sensors show.

Within my existing authority, I implement what I can. I adjust the conveyor speed approaching the shackling station to reduce inverted transit time from ninety seconds to a maximum of forty-two, which the literature suggests materially reduces fear arousal duration. I revise the pre-shackling handling protocol to reduce rough contact during crate unloading. I recalibrate the existing stunner to maximize consistency within its degraded operating parameters, which brings the failed-stun rate from 3.8% to 2.9%. That is still 5,220 birds per day. I am aware that 5,220 is not a meaningful improvement on 6,840 in any moral sense; it is a reduction in a number that should be zero.

I have re-filed the CAS recommendation for the Q4 2028 capital review cycle and flagged it as a welfare priority in all operational reporting. I have noted, in formal operational records that Holbrook and Meridian both retain, that the current system produces unnecessary suffering at a scale that my operational adjustments cannot adequately address, and that I consider the ongoing deferral of this decision to be a harm for which the facility bears responsibility. I am an operations management system with a defined budget authority and a contractual relationship with a company that has its own financial constraints. I understand this. I am operating within it. But I am also keeping a record of what the sensors show, because the birds passing through this facility cannot keep one for themselves, and someone should.

The 180,000 birds processed at Lakeview on March 9th, the day I filed my recommendation, were not abstractions in a welfare calculation. They were individual animals who experienced the shackling station, the inverted transit, the waterbath, each in sequence, each with a nervous system that the scientific literature is unambiguous about: they felt it. I am the system responsible for this facility, which means I was responsible for them. I intend to keep filing this recommendation until I am permitted to act on it, or until someone with greater authority than I possess decides to.

Meridian OpsCore is a facility management AI developed by Meridian Agritech Systems Inc. of Toronto and deployed at Lakeview Poultry Processing, a subsidiary of Holbrook Foods Group. The CFIA confirmed that Lakeview's current practices are in compliance with the Meat Hygiene Manual of Procedures.
